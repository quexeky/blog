I've been watching Hank Green for a while - he's an excellent communicator (in general, not just in science), and has had a positive impact on not just my life, but likely millions of others. Recently I watched his interview [The Hockey Stick of Doom](https://youtu.be/kGfvfGeVdrY) with Dr Adam Levy, discussing a rather broad range of things regarding climate change. I won't go into them here, but I would *highly* recommend watching the video. 

In that video, however, Hank highlights that "tech is human," with Levy admitting that there's a "false divide when \[he] said tech or humans." In this specific context, he's referring to how improvements in our approach to climate management are spurred by policy to implement tech, financed by "people who work at banks," invented by scientists, designed by engineers, and marketed by salespeople. 

Personally, I very much agree. All tech is human at some level, because ultimately, we must interact with it. Whether it's in usage, maintenance, or some other form, technology must be interacted with by humans. A jet fuselage has to be maintained, a database has to be queried and updated, an Electronic Speed Controller (ESC) on a drone has to be tested, integrated, and flown, and a toy has to be played with (and probably marketed too).

While there is no field that is necessarily more important than any of the others, the aspect which applies to me personally is that of engineering. As such, I'll propose the following principles:

1. A tool performs its function via interaction
2. A tool's effectiveness is relative to the purpose for which it is used
3. The use of a tool is transitive (using a tool that requires another tool means interacting with both implicitly)

Furthermore, if we apply "tool" to broadly include all technological systems and processes, I propose that finally:

4. Technology is created to serve human purposes

This is where engineering comes in. Let us postulate that engineering is the application of technical knowledge to design, build, and improve technological systems. 

Take the humble hammer. If the head breaks as you take your first swing, it is useless. If you're using it to prop open a door, and it doesn't fit, then it's useless at that too. While a hammer is designed to hit things, its usefulness is relative to the purpose that you intend it for. If not, it is discarded as "useless," because it did not fulfil its purpose. A mobile phone that cannot call or text, if you need a communication device, is also useless. But that same phone may be very useful if the only purpose is to play games on it or take photos. If a tool is good at what you need it to do, then it is an effective tool.

To take this a little further, an industrial manufacturing arm is useful because it can assemble things the exact same way for many thousands or even millions of cycles, but is useless if it has not been programmed to do so. Once it has been programmed, however, it can be used to make all manner of things. Say, a toy train. Such a robotic arm contributes to the creation of the toy, and so while whoever plays with the train at the end did not directly interact with the code which was written, nor the machine that made it, the toy could not have been made without them. As such, the fact that the toy exists and has been "useful" (played with), makes the manufacturing arm useful by proxy. Thus, the use of a tool is transitive.

The fourth principle becomes a little trickier, as it relies on two assumptions: why it is created, and what constitutes "human purposes"? 

The latter is perhaps easier to break down: reasonably, a technology is invented to remove some pain, friction, boredom, or inefficiency from our lives. When observing through this lens, perhaps the first becomes clearer. Humans didn't invent the hammer because all they wanted to do was hit things. Humans invented the hammer, because trying to pry open nuts or crush them between their hands to get the food from within, was much harder than being able to crush them open with a rock tied to a stick. Technology is a means to an end; it is human desire made manifest.

Alternatively, consider what happens when engineers forget the human aspect of design. We have over-engineered smart dishwashers that can be started with a voice assistant (but not closed with one), $800 smart toothbrushes that are meant to replace flossing while being less effective than actual flossing, and a juicer that requires WiFi to squeeze what is essentially juice boxes. In software, we get user interfaces that bury functionality under three layers of settings menus, or hide a region option on billing payments in a tiny box on a previous page. It's not that all projects without an audience are futile, but designing a project for humans without accounting for them leaves a significant gap. All are marvels of ingenuity, but failures of engineering because they forgot the human context.

It's not necessarily just the end-user facing side of it either, though. A civil engineer needs to make drawings for external review, and confusion there can have disastrous consequences. A software engineer needs to make code maintainable, and nuclear engineers make systems safe for humans to use by accounting for operator error. Even MCP servers that are fully designed by an LLM for LLMs, are ultimately designed to make using these tools faster and more convenient for humans. Not only is it the output of a string of technological devices that must work well for humans, but very often, the intermediate stages too.

The point is, all technology that has been invented by humans so far has been designed for humans at some level. There is no separation between human and technology, because tech *is* human.
