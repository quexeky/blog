I've been watching Hank Green for a while - he's an excellent communicator (in general, not just in science), and has had a positive impact on not just my life, but likely millions of others. Recently I watched his interview [The Hockey Stick of Doom](https://youtu.be/kGfvfGeVdrY) with Dr Adam Levy, discussing a rather broad range of things regarding Climate Change. I won't go into them here, but I would *highly* recommend watching the video. 

In that video, however, he highlights that "tech is human," and that there's a "false divide when \[he] said tech or humans." In this specific context, he's referring to how improvements in our approach to climate management are spurred by policy to implement tech, financed by "people who work at banks," invented by scientists, designed by engineers, and marketed by salespeople. 

Personally, I very much agree - all tech must be human at some level, because at some level, a human must interact with it - whether it's in usage or maintenance, almost all (probably not absolutely all, but that's the vast minority) technology must be interacted with by humans. A jet fuselage has to be maintained, a database has to be developed with, an ESC has to be flown (in the case of drones) and tested, and a toy has to be played with (and probably marketed too), all of which are done with a human interacting with the specific component. 

While there is no field that is necessarily more important than any of the others (to each their own, though), the aspect which applies to me personally is that of Engineering. As such, I'll propose the following axioms:

1. A tool requires interaction to be useful
2. An optimal tool is optimally useful
3. Interacting with a tool which itself interacts with another tool requires interacting by the other tool by implication
4. The creation of a tool requires interaction
5. All technology is a tool
