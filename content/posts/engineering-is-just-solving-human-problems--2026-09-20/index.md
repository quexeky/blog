I've been watching Hank Green for a while - he's an excellent communicator (in general, not just in science), and has had a positive impact on not just my life, but likely millions of others. Recently I watched his interview [The Hockey Stick of Doom](https://youtu.be/kGfvfGeVdrY) with Dr Adam Levy, discussing a rather broad range of things regarding climate change. I won't go into them here, but I would *highly* recommend watching the video. 

In that video, however, Hank highlights that "tech is human," with Levy admitting that there's a "false divide when \[he] said tech or humans." In this specific context, he's referring to how improvements in our approach to climate management are spurred by policy to implement tech, financed by "people who work at banks," invented by scientists, designed by engineers, and marketed by salespeople. 

Personally, I very much agree - all tech must be human at some level, because at some level, a human must interact with it - whether it's in usage or maintenance, almost all (probably not absolutely all, but that's the vast minority) technology must be interacted with by humans. A jet fuselage has to be maintained, a database has to be developed with, an Electronic Speed Controller (ESC) has to be flown (in the case of drones) and tested, and a toy has to be played with (and probably marketed too), all of which are done with a human interacting with the specific component. 

While there is no field that is necessarily more important than any of the others (to each their own, though), the aspect which applies to me personally is that of Engineering. As such, I'll propose the following principles:

1. A tool performs its function via interaction
2. A tool's effectiveness is relative to the purpose for which it is used
3. The use of a tool is transitive*

\*In that using a tool which in some way requires another tool, means interacting with both transitively

Furthermore, assume that all technology is a tool (a truism), and finally:

4. Technology is created to serve human purposes

This is where Engineering comes in. Let us propose that Engineering is the application of technical knowledge to design, build, and improve technological systems. 

Take one of the simplest tools: a hammer. It is not a very good hammer if the handle breaks as you take your first swing, nor is it useful if it bounces off something you have tried to hit. Similarly, if you wanted to use it to prop something up or open, and it isn't the right fit, then it's useless at that too. While a hammer is designed to hit things, its usefulness is relative to the purpose that you intend it for. A hammer that is used to hit something hard and simply breaks, is discarded as "useless," because it did not fulfil its purpose. A mobile phone that cannot call or text, if you need a communication device, is also useless. But that same phone may be very useful if the only purpose is to play games on it or take photos. If a tool is good at what you need it to do, then it is an effective tool.

To take this a little further, an industrial manufacturing arm is useful because it can assemble things the exact same way for many thousands or even millions of cycles, but is useless if it has not been programmed to do so. Once it has been programmed, however, it can be used to make all manner of things. Say, a toy train. Such a robotic arm contributes to the creation of the toy, and so while whoever plays with the train at the end did not directly interact with the code which was written, nor the machine that made it, the toy could not have been made without them. As such, the fact that the toy exists and has been "useful" (played with), makes the manufacturing arm useful by proxy. Thus, the use of a tool is transitive.

The fourth principle becomes a little harder to propose, as it relies on two assumptions: why it is created, and what are "human purposes"? 

The latter is perhaps easier to break down: reasonably, a technology is invented to remove some pain, friction, boredom, or inefficiency from our lives. When observing through this lens, perhaps the first becomes clearer. Humans didn't invent the hammer because all they wanted to do was hit things. Humans invented the hammer, because trying to pry open nuts or crush them between their hands to get the food from within, was much harder than being able to crush it open with a rock tied to a stick. Technology is a means to an end; it is human desire made manifest. 

Alternatively, consider what happens when engineers forgets the human aspect of design. Over-engineered smart dishwashers that can be started with a voice assistant, $800 smart toothbrushes that is meant to replace flossing while being less effective than actual flossing, and a juicer that requires WiFi to squeeze what is essentially juice boxes. In software, we get user interfaces that bury functionality under three layers of settings menus, or have a region option on billing payments that are in a tiny box in a previous page in a flow that is dedicated to just billing. It's not that all projects without an audience are futile, but designing a project for humans without accounting for them leaves a significant gap. All are marvels of ingenuity, but the lack of context reflects on them poorly.

It's not necessarily just the end-user facing side of it either, though. A civil engineer needs to make drawings for external review, and confusion there can have disastrous consequences. A software engineer needs to make code maintainable, and nuclear engineers make systems safe for humans to use by accounting for operator error. Even MCP servers that are fully designed by an LLM for LLMs, are ultimately designed to make using these tools faster and more convenient for humans. Not only is it the output of a string of technological devices that must work well for humans, but very often, the intermediate stages too.

The point is, all technology that has been invented by humans so far, has been designed for humans at some level. There is no separation between human and technology, because tech *is* human.
